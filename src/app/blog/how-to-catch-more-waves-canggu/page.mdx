<h2 className="font-heading text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-10 mb-4">How to Catch More Waves: It Is a Skill, Not Luck</h2>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Catching a wave is a physics problem before it is a surfing one. A wave will only pick you up if your board is already travelling close to the wave's speed at the moment it arrives — otherwise the water simply rolls underneath you and moves on. Everything that raises your wave count is really just a way of solving that one equation: be in the place where the wave has the most push (position), start early enough that the board is already moving (timing), and paddle hard enough to close the gap (power). Riders who catch fifteen waves to your three are not paddling harder in some heroic sense; they have quietly solved position and timing so that very little power is even needed.
</p>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  That is good news, because position and timing cost nothing and improve the same afternoon you work on them. Below I have broken the three levers down in the order that gives you the most extra waves for the least effort — and, importantly, in the order I would fix them for you if you paddled out with me tomorrow.
</p>

<h2 className="font-heading text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-10 mb-4">Position: Being in the Right Place Before the Wave Arrives</h2>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  If you change only one thing, change where you sit. The overwhelmingly common fault is sitting too far out — waiting beyond the break for the perfect set, watching catchable wave after catchable wave peel through in front of you, and only ever scrambling for the leftovers. Move in. Sit at the peak, the point where the wave first stands up and begins to break, and if anything err slightly to the inside of where instinct tells you to be. That is where the wave has the most energy and the steepest, easiest push.
</p>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Holding that position takes active work, especially in Canggu, where the current walks you steadily down the beach the whole time you sit still. Pick two fixed landmarks on shore that line up — a particular tree against a particular building — and glance at them often; the moment they slide apart, you have drifted and need to paddle back. On a beach break like <Link href="/batu-bolong-surf" className="text-accent-blue hover:underline">Batu Bolong</Link> the peak itself also migrates with the tide and the shifting sandbanks, so yesterday's perfect spot can be twenty metres away today. Knowing where to sit is inseparable from <Link href="/blog/how-to-read-a-wave-canggu" className="text-accent-blue hover:underline">reading the wave in Canggu</Link> before it arrives — the two skills are really one, and they are what separate a busy session from a frustrating one.
</p>

<div className="my-8 border-t border-border-subtle" />

<h2 className="font-heading text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-10 mb-4">Timing: Start Paddling Before It Feels Right</h2>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Once you are in the right spot, the next lever is when you commit. Almost every missed wave I watch comes from starting to paddle too late. By the time you can feel the tail lifting, the wave is already leaving without you. The correct moment feels alarmingly early: turn and go while the wave is still several metres behind you, put in three or four genuinely committed strokes to get the board moving, then one or two more hard ones as it reaches you to match its pace. You want to already be sliding when the wave gets there, so it only has to nudge you along rather than heave a dead-stopped board up to speed.
</p>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Committing that early is uncomfortable precisely because you cannot see the wave behind you, so beginners hesitate, take a couple of tentative strokes, look back to check — and by then it has gone. The cure is to stop watching the wave and start watching the swell approach from further out, so you know it is coming and can turn with confidence. When the timing finally clicks, the pop-up has to follow instantly, which is why a slow or knees-first stand-up quietly costs you waves too; if that is your sticking point, the mechanics are in our guide to <Link href="/blog/how-to-pop-up-surfing-bali" className="text-accent-blue hover:underline">how to pop up on a surfboard</Link>.
</p>

<h2 className="font-heading text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-10 mb-4">Paddle Power: The Engine Nobody Trains</h2>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Position and timing reduce how much power you need, but they do not remove it, and weak paddling caps everyone's wave count eventually. The fix is technique before fitness. Reach well forward and sink your arm to the shoulder — a deep stroke that pulls water from ahead of you to behind your hip moves the board far more than a shallow slap at the surface. Alternate cleanly, keep your legs together and still rather than kicking up spray, and above all keep the board trimmed flat: lie centred so the nose rides an inch or two clear of the water, because a buried nose ploughs and a sunk tail drags, and both bleed the speed you are working to build.
</p>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Real paddle fitness, the kind that lets you go wave after wave without your arms turning to concrete, is built by paddling and almost nothing else. This is one of the quiet reasons a week of consecutive surfing changes people so much more than the same hours spread across months at home — the endurance compounds day to day. We lay out that rhythm in our guide to <Link href="/blog/how-long-to-learn-surfing-bali" className="text-accent-blue hover:underline">how long it takes to learn to surf in Bali</Link>, and it applies just as much to catching more waves as to standing up for the first time.
</p>

<h2 className="font-heading text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-10 mb-4">Ride Enough Board — The Fix Almost Nobody Wants to Hear</h2>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  The single fastest way to catch more waves is also the one improvers resist most: ride more volume. A board's volume is its float, and float is forgiveness — a bigger, thicker board paddles faster, sits higher, catches waves earlier and lets you get into them from further back with much less effort. Surfers who feel like they never catch anything are very often simply under-boarded, having copied the short, thin boards they see advanced surfers riding at the peak. Those boards catch fewer waves on purpose; the skill of the rider hides it.
</p>

<p className="text-text-secondary leading-relaxed mb-4">
  While you are still building wave count, ride the most volume you can stand — a longboard or a generous soft-top will comfortably double your waves in a session compared with a shortboard, and every one of those extra waves is a repetition that sharpens your position and timing. There is absolutely a time to step the size down, but it comes after your paddling and positioning are dialled, not before. On any <Link href="/surf-lessons-canggu" className="text-accent-blue hover:underline">surf lesson in Canggu</Link> the board is provided and matched to your size and level, which removes the guesswork completely — and it is one reason people catch more waves in a coached session than they do out alone on their own gear.
</p>

  One honest caveat sits over all of this: more waves for you should not mean snaking them from other people. As your positioning improves you will find yourself sitting deeper, closer to the peak, exactly where right-of-way is decided — and getting that wrong turns a good session sour fast and, in a crowded Canggu lineup, occasionally dangerous.
